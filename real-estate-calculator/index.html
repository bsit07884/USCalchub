How Real Estate Commissions Work in 2026

Real estate commissions are typically the single largest expense a homeowner faces when selling a property. Traditionally calculated as a percentage of the final home sale price, this fee is designed to compensate the real estate professionals who market, negotiate, and facilitate the transaction. But how exactly is this fee distributed, and who is responsible for paying it?

Who Pays the Commission: Buyer or Seller?

Historically, the seller has been responsible for paying the entire real estate commission out of their home's equity. This total fee was usually split down the middle between the seller's listing agent and the buyer's agent. However, recent legal shifts have changed this dynamic. While sellers still primarily pay their own listing agent, they are no longer strictly obligated to cover the buyer's agent fees, opening up new avenues for negotiation.

Are Commission Rates Negotiable?

Absolutely. There is no federal or state law that sets a standard or mandatory real estate commission rate. Everything is negotiable. Depending on the local housing market conditions, the condition of your home, and the level of service provided by the brokerage, you can often negotiate these rates down to protect your equity. Many discount brokerages even offer 1% to 2% listing fees in exchange for fewer premium marketing services.

Average 2026 Commission Rates

While rates vary by city and state, the national average total real estate commission in 2026 hovers around 5.0% to 5.8%. Typically, this is broken down into approximately 2.5% to 3.0% for the listing agent and a similar percentage for the buyer's agent. Understanding these averages gives you a solid baseline when interviewing potential realtors for your property.

How This Calculation Works

2026 NAR Settlement Impact

Since August 2024, buyers and sellers negotiate agent commissions separately. The average total dropped from 6% to 5.70%. Savvy sellers achieve 4.0-4.5% in competitive markets.

What Is Not Included

Title insurance, escrow fees, transfer taxes, staging costs, and mortgage payoff are not included. Add 1-3% for total closing costs.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

1. How do I calculate my realtor's commission fee manually?

To calculate the fee manually, multiply the final sale price of your home by the total commission percentage. For example, if you sell a house for $400,000 at a 5% commission rate, you multiply 400,000 by 0.05, resulting in a $20,000 total commission fee.

2. What percentage do most realtors charge?

Most traditional real estate agents charge a total commission ranging between 5% and 6% of the home's sale price. This total is usually divided between the seller's agent and the buyer's agent, often resulting in a 2.5% to 3% split for each professional.

3. How much commission do you get on a $300,000 house?

Assuming a standard total commission rate of 6%, the total fee on a $300,000 house would be $18,000. If this is split evenly, both the listing agent's brokerage and the buyer's agent's brokerage would receive $9,000 each.

4. Is 2% a good commission?

Yes, securing a 2% listing commission is generally considered a good deal for the seller, as it sits below the traditional 2.5% to 3% average. Many modern brokerages and highly competitive agents will accept a 2% rate, allowing the seller to retain more equity.

5. Does the buyer ever pay real estate commission?

Yes, it is becoming more common. If a seller chooses not to offer a commission to the buyer's agent, the buyer may be responsible for paying their agent directly out of pocket, or they may negotiate for the seller to cover it through closing cost concessions.

6. What's the difference between listing agent and buyer's agent commission?

The listing agent's commission pays the professional who markets the home, hosts open houses, and represents the seller's interests. The buyer's agent commission compensates the professional who brings a qualified buyer, shows them the property, and helps them negotiate the purchase offer.

7. Can I sell my home without paying full commission (FSBO)?

Yes, you can list your home "For Sale By Owner" (FSBO) to avoid paying a listing agent's commission entirely. However, you may still need to offer a competitive commission to a buyer's agent if you want them to show your property to their clients.

8. How has the 2026 NAR settlement changed real estate commissions?

The landmark NAR settlement decoupled commissions, meaning offers of compensation to buyer agents are strictly banned from the MLS, giving sellers absolute freedom in what they pay. For a complete breakdown, read our guide on the Realtor Commission Calculator 2026 NAR Settlement rules.
